# Product character

Read this before writing a direction's tone and visual-language answers, to set the product's character, translate it and check it.

Methods for setting character rest on practitioner experience; research covers whole-screen perception better than specific traits.

## 1. Gather the inputs

Read each on its own first:

- What the business wants to convey: its vision and any existing brand, whose words are often too broad to guide screens.
- How users want to feel, from the taste rules and the jobs.
- What the category and its competitors already convey.
- The audience: preferred visual complexity and colourfulness differ by gender, education and country (well tested, as group averages); prefer what is known of this audience over its demographics.

When inputs conflict, write which one leads and why; the sources give no ranking.

## 2. Weigh the attribute set

- How many words? Sources range from three to seven, untested. Do they tell the whole story together?
- Can each word be broken? Bound it by what it must not become, or by a spectrum position, so it can be checked.
- Does each word describe the product, or its imagined owner? One thesis on physical products found product traits perceived more consistently (weak evidence for software).
- Could users tell every pair apart? Near-synonyms clustered in one preliminary survey (weak evidence).
- Where does the character shift with the situation, and where does it hold?

## 3. Describe the options space

No tested mapping from a word to a value exists; translation is judgement, checked by critique. Place each candidate on these dimensions:

- **Carriers:** which of colour, type, shape, space, motion and copy carry each attribute, and how strongly.
- **Lead attribute:** which word dominates. Practitioners explore several directions, each led by a different word.
- **Whole-screen position:** visual complexity, typicality for the category and colourfulness predict first-glance appeal, judged within a fraction of a second (well tested, on static web pages). A look less typical of its category pays a first-glance cost, while novelty also raises preference and shared category styling cannot identify a brand (`register.md`); weigh both sides as `REASONING.md` weighs familiar and new choices.

Sources disagree on selection: take a whole direction by its dominant attribute, or combine elements across directions. Both are open; after a mix, check the result still carries every attribute.

They also disagree on whether appeal makes an interface seem usable: correlational studies say yes, a controlled experiment found usability shaping perceived beauty after use. Decide how much weight first-glance appeal gets against appeal after repeated use.

## 4. Test the choice

Judge character on a composed screen: elements read differently alone than in context (weak evidence), and in one small study only one of five intended characters landed.

- **With users:** a word-choice test (targets fixed beforehand, mixed with negative and neutral words including the bounds, order randomised; count who picks at least one target), opposite-adjective rating scales, a first-impression test timed to the screen's complexity, or a validated appeal questionnaire whose facets match the written attributes. No source gives a pass threshold or sample size. Compare what users say with what they do.
- **Without users:** review against the written attributes and bounds; a reviewer who has not seen the targets picks words from the same mixed list (untested).

Contrast and legibility floors live in `CRAFT.md`; character sits above them.

## 5. Write down the reasons

- The attributes, each with its bound, and where it shifts.
- Which input led when they conflicted.
- Each attribute's carriers, for the tone field.
- The lead attribute, and the whole-screen position with the audience it was judged for.
- How it was checked and what came back, or that it is unchecked.
