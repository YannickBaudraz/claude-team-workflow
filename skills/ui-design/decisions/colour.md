# Colour

Read this before choosing a product's hues, how much colour each screen carries, and what each colour means.

Floors: `CRAFT.md`. Brand presence: `register.md`. Checking a feeling: `character.md`.

## Inputs

- Fixed constraints: a sector requirement or a mandated palette.
- The brand's colours, if any, and the part people recognise in each (hue, lightness, saturation); without guidelines, colours already carrying recognition.
- The jobs colour could do: action, selection, focus, links, status severities, object states, categories, identity.
- How colourful the content is (images, charts, user picks), which bears on how much colour the interface itself carries.
- The audience's locales, colour vision, light and screens.

## Criteria and evidence

- **Conflicts.** The floor holds. Beyond it, established meanings, the brand and the taste rules can each give ground, and no source states an order as a rule. Documented cases kept each established meaning on its own hue and fitted the brand, retuning its lightness and saturation or limiting where it appears; sometimes both sides gave ground. Write which input leads here and why, naming any taste rule involved.
- **Attention.** Well tested in lab search: a colour draws the eye by how far it differs from its neighbours and how uniform they are. The pull weakens for users with a clear goal, though a consistent colour code stays findable. Untested on dense application screens.
- **Hue meaning.** Weak evidence: colour-emotion links, measured with colour words, are broadly shared across nations yet shift with language and context; single-hue effects on behaviour have largely failed to hold up. Use meaning as a tiebreaker, tested with the audience.
- **Colour vision.** The commonest deficiency confuses the hue pair most used for opposite statuses.

## Options space

- **Hue count**, three ways: on a typical screen, in reserve for states, in the categorical set. Advice on hue counts varies widely and likely counts different things (an inference).
- **Method.** Sources disagree: start from a colour-wheel scheme, or build a tonal scale per hue.
- **Share and emphasis**: which jobs get colour, at which level from subtle to bold, how many bold elements a region holds. Fixed share ratios have no evidence.
- **Surface colour**: neutral, tinted (towards which hue) or coloured outright, weighed against the coloured components on top; practitioner judgement only.
- **Tonal character**: overall contrast level above the floors, a character choice weighed against the readers and their light; whether text and neutrals carry hue. Practitioners report that lower contrast costs legibility and confidence; no study ties contrast range to perceived qualities.
- **Semantic roles**, derived from the states and severities users must tell apart at a glance, in severity order. Hues follow learned, culture-bound conventions.
- **Brand and status on one hue.** Restrict the brand hue (less presence), shift the status hue (weaker convention), or merge action with positive status on purpose ("do this" and "good" blur). All are in use, none tested with users.
- **Categorical and user colours**: whether categories get a named set without meaning; equal lightness (hues swap freely and none looks ranked, but they are harder to tell apart) or varied lightness (easier to tell apart, some look ranked); whether their component form differs from status; whether pickers are limited to the set. Distinguishing categories competes with contrast.

## Testing

- Render the main real screens in every theme the product ships, in bright and dim light, on several displays.
- Check misreadings: muted control as disabled, brand or category as status, decoration as link.
- Check that hues meant to differ also differ in lightness; view through a colour-vision simulation.
- Test intended emotional readings with the audience.

## Recording

Add to the direction:

- each role's purpose, the jobs it stays out of, its hue and the input that set it;
- which input won each conflict, and what the other gave up;
- the three hue counts, emphasis per job, how many bold elements each region holds;
- each claim that a colour signals a quality, as a bet until users confirm it.
